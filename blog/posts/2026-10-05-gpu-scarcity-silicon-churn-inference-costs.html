GPU Scarcity and Custom Silicon: The Compute Churn Behind AI

AR Akhil Reddy Danda · 5th October, 2026 · 2 min read

The silicon story for AI in late 2026 is plain: GPU/TPU supply is still the bottleneck. Nvidia’s new H100N and Google’s CloudTPUv7 are hot, but you can’t get enough for big workloads. That means custom ASICs—like Groq’s inferencing chips and Meta’s homebrew silicon—are getting traction, but they’re mostly closed to outsiders.

Datacenter Build-Outs & Inference Costs

Major clouds (AWS, Azure, Google) are racing to build datacenters with more efficient cooling and power, but inference costs for LLMs haven’t dropped much. Most engineers still feel the pain: priced out of large models unless you have volume or special access. Even small workloads can get expensive if you don’t optimize batch size and quantization.

Energy and Supply Chain Realities

Energy draw is the other wall: AI datacenters are pushing grids harder than ever, leading to priority battles between AI, crypto, and regular web services. For engineers, this means “serverless” AI isn’t free. You have to consider latency, cost, and region—especially if your users are global or you’re shipping real-time features. The chip supply chain is still fragile; any hiccup in Taiwan, Korea, or the US means delays for everyone.

What Matters for Engineers

If you ship AI in 2026, you need to know: a) which clouds actually have silicon, b) how to budget for inference spikes, and c) how to optimize your models (quantize, prune, batch). The compute landscape is not abstract—it’s the practical ceiling on what you can build. Until there’s a true silicon glut, every engineer should treat compute as a first-class resource, not an afterthought.
