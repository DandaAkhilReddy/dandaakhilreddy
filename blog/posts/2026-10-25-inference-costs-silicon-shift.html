Inference Costs, Silicon Shakeups, and What Engineers Need To Rethink Now

AR Akhil Reddy Danda · 25th October, 2026 · 2 min read

GPU shortages used to be a headline; now it's just background noise. What's hotter: custom AI silicon rolling out from NVIDIA, Google, and a dozen startups—plus the rise of open foundry efforts to sidestep supply bottlenecks. Meanwhile, inference costs for state-of-the-art models are still stubbornly high, even as power usage becomes a boardroom issue.

The Real Impact: More Choice (and Fragmentation)

Engineers deploying real-world AI now have to pick between: (1) standard NVIDIA/AMD GPUs, (2) Google TPUs, (3) AWS Trainium/Inferentia, (4) specialized edge chips, or (5) custom accelerators from new kids like Tenstorrent and Cerebras. Each choice means wrestling with different toolchains, deployment quirks, and sometimes rewriting operator kernels to fit the hardware. This isn't a future problem—if you're building AI products, you're making these decisions now.

Why does inference still cost so much? It's not just chip pricing. It's datacenter power, cooling, yield, and every hop in the supply chain. With the AI model-of-the-month club, even slightly better inference efficiency (in watts or ms) translates to millions saved—or lost—per year. We're already seeing startups go under because their models cost too much to run at scale, even with clever quantization and distillation.

Why This Matters for Engineers

If you want your AI to actually ship—not just demo—become obsessed with hardware-aware engineering. Can you squeeze your model for the silicon you can actually get? Can you trade tiny drops in accuracy for huge drops in cost? Are you ready to replatform if a supply chain hiccup hits China or Taiwan? Every engineer needs to track hardware trends, not just model architectures. The winners will be the teams that unify model, code, and hardware in their workflow.
