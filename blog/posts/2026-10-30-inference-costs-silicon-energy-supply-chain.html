AI Compute in 2026: Inference Costs, Custom Silicon, and the Real Energy Problem

AR Akhil Reddy Danda · 30th October, 2026 · 2 min read

Anyone shipping AI today knows it’s not just about how fast your model runs—it’s about whether you can afford to run it at all. NVIDIA’s new Blackwell chips are powerful but scarce; Google’s TPUv6 is optimized for inference, and custom silicon from startups (think Groq, Tenstorrent) is driving down latency for certain workloads. But the real bottleneck is cost.

Inference Isn’t Cheap—And It’s Getting Pricier

The average inference cost for large models has gone up thanks to datacenter power limits and supply chain snags. You can’t just throw GPUs at the problem, because everyone is fighting for slots. Engineers are optimizing models like it’s 2017 again: quantization, distillation, and clever routing are back in vogue.

Energy and Datacenter Build-Outs

Microsoft, Google, and AWS are all pouring billions into new datacenters, but the build-out pace is slower than demand. Energy is the limiting factor—more than hardware. If your AI tool runs at scale, you must profile power draw, batch inference, and offload to custom chips where possible.

Why Supply Chain Matters

Chip supply disruptions mean you can’t always rely on ‘just-in-time’ scaling. Engineers need to think about fallback model sizes, multi-cloud failover, and hybrid serving. If your AI system depends on a specific chip, you’re risking downtime—or astronomical costs—when the next shortage hits.

Bottom line: compute isn’t abstract. It’s the concrete bottleneck for anyone shipping AI. If you aren’t tracking inference cost, energy, and supply chain risk, you’re building on sand.
