Samsung’s 3nm Gate-All-Around Hits Mass Production: Why FETs Just Got Interesting Again

AR Akhil Reddy Danda · 28th September, 2026 · 2 min read

The transistor’s not dead, but the old FinFET is on hospice care. Samsung’s 3nm GAA (Gate-All-Around) process hitting mass production means we’re entering a new era for scaling—one that doesn’t only shrink, but reshapes device physics. GAA wraps the gate material entirely around the channel, meaning way better electrostatic control. So why should AI engineers and system builders actually care?

Performance and Power, Not Just Hype

First, GAA enables up to 20% lower supply voltages and—honestly more impressive in practice—about 30% better power efficiency versus 5nm. For on-device AI and edge hardware, that’s not just a spec sheet brag. It’s more AI ops per watt, longer battery life, and, crucially, the ability to pack more specialized accelerators (NPU, ISP, even RISC-V cores) into the same thermal envelope. You get smaller, faster, cooler chips. That fundamentally expands what’s possible in mobile, AR/VR, and even datacenter edge deployments.

Practical Yields and Foundry Competition

Yields have always been the boogeyman for new nodes, but Samsung claims their 3nm GAA yields are now competitive with TSMC’s 3nm FinFET, thanks to a clever nanosheet width tunability trick. For design engineers, that means fewer respins and a realistic path to tapeout for custom silicon and ASICs. We’re likely to see the first commercial SoCs with this tech later this year—Samsung, yes, but also Chinese hyperscalers looking to bypass TSMC bottlenecks.

Bottom line: GAA at 3nm is here, and it’s not just another shrink. It’s the start of a new Moore’s Law for system design—if you’re architecting AI or edge solutions, pay attention.
